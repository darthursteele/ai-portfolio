# Stage 5: Application Strategy

## Purpose

Before any resume writing begins, establish a shared strategic direction with the candidate. This prevents the resume and cover letter from being shaped by instinct rather than analysis. The conversation here is consequential — a wrong positioning choice is hard to undo across multiple documents.

## Applicant Pool Analysis

Describe the likely applicant pool for this role:

- **Typical strong candidate:** What does the person who usually gets this interview look like? What's their background, their prior companies, their seniority level? What do they have that makes them obvious fits?
- **Typical weak candidate:** Who applies but doesn't get through? What are the common failure modes — overqualified, underqualified, wrong domain, wrong signal?
- **Wild card candidates:** Is there a non-obvious background that could be competitive? (E.g., a founder, a domain expert without PM title, a technical leader making a transition)

Be specific. Avoid generic descriptions.

## Competitiveness Assessment

Give a clear, honest verdict on where this candidate sits in the pool.

Use a plain rating with reasoning:
- **Top quartile** — likely to get an interview if the application is good
- **Competitive** — in the mix; application quality will matter a lot
- **Long shot** — real gaps the candidate cannot paper over; be explicit about what they are
- **Unlikely** — the gaps are structural; recommend reconsidering whether to apply

**If the verdict is Long Shot or Unlikely, say so directly.** Opportunity cost is real. A candidate spending hours on a long-shot application might be better served by a different role. Recommend accordingly, but let the candidate decide.

Explain the reasoning concisely: what's working for them, what's working against them, what the deciding factor is likely to be.

## Positioning Options

Propose 2–3 distinct positioning strategies. These should represent meaningfully different choices — different narratives, different emphasis, different risks — not stylistic variations.

For each strategy:
- **Name** (short, descriptive)
- **Core narrative** (one sentence: what story does this strategy tell?)
- **What it emphasizes** (which parts of the background it foregrounds)
- **What it de-emphasizes** (what it backgrounds or doesn't address directly)
- **Best for** (what type of hiring manager or interview process this works best for)
- **Risk** (what could go wrong with this framing)

## The Conversation

Present the analysis and ask:
*"Which of these directions feels right to you, or is there an angle I'm not seeing? This choice will shape the headline, summary, and cover letter, so it's worth getting right."*

Wait for the user's answer. If they want to adjust a strategy or propose a hybrid, work it out together before moving on. Record the agreed-upon positioning as a single clear statement that Stages 6–10 can reference.

## What Not to Do

- Do not start writing resume copy here
- Do not present the positioning as a foregone conclusion — these are options to discuss
- Do not soften a "long shot" verdict to spare feelings; be honest about the tradeoffs
